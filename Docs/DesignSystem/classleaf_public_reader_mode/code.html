Lecture 14: Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution & Arenium Ions

Electrophilic aromatic substitution (SEAr) is the hallmark reaction of aromatic compounds. Because benzene possesses an exceptionally stable cloud of delocalized π electrons with 36 kcal/mol of resonance stabilization energy, it resists typical addition reactions that would otherwise disrupt aromaticity permanently.

1 Generation of the Electrophile

The aromatic π electron cloud is a relatively weak nucleophile. Ordinary electrophiles like molecular bromine (Br2) or chlorine (Cl2) are not potent enough to elicit attack. A potent Lewis acid catalyst—typically FeBr3 or AlCl3—must be deployed to coordinate with the halogen, polarizing the covalent bond and generating a fiercely electrophilic species.

“The catalyst does not merely assist; it generates the transient super-electrophile required to overcome the ground-state aromatic stabilization barrier.”

2 Nucleophilic Attack by the π System

In the rate-determining step (RDS), two π electrons from the benzene ring attack the electrophile E+. This generates a non-aromatic, resonance-stabilized carbocation known as an arenium ion (or Wheland intermediate / σ-complex).

3 Deprotonation and Aromatization

Because re-establishing the 6π aromatic sextet provides immense thermodynamic driving force, the arenium ion rapidly sheds an sp3-bound proton. The conjugate Lewis base abstracts H+, restoring full aromaticity and regenerating the acid catalyst in a fast, highly exergonic step.

eco Key Takeaways
  • check_circle Substitution over addition: Benzene prefers substitution to preserve its 36 kcal/mol resonance stabilization.
  • check_circle RDS: Formation of the non-aromatic arenium ion is the slow kinetic rate-limiting barrier.
  • check_circle Regeneration: Deprotonation is ultrafast and reconstitutes the Lewis acid catalyst.
